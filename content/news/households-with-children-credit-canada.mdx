Households with children report the sharpest financial pressure of any group measured in the survey **Equifax Canada released on August 6, 2026** — and the findings arrive in the month when family spending peaks.

**In this article:** the family gap · what is driving it · the back-to-school collision · the support that exists · why this matters · key takeaways · sources

## The gap for households with children

Equifax Canada surveyed **1,532 Canadians aged 18 to 65 between July 17 and 19, 2026**, using Leger's online panel, with a reported margin of error of **±2.5 per cent, 19 times out of 20**.

| Measure | All respondents | **Households with children** |
|---|---|---|
| **Spending more than a year ago** | 40% | **51%** |
| **Using more credit for essentials** | 29% | **42%** |
| Expect only minimum credit card payments | 25% | **33%** |
| Concerned about supporting family members | 26% | **45%** |

Every gap is wide, and two are very wide. **Households with children are 13 percentage points more likely to be putting groceries and utilities on credit**, and **19 points more likely to worry about supporting family members financially.**

**Rebecca Oakes, Vice-President of Advanced Analytics at Equifax Canada**, said in the release that "the results point to mounting financial pressure for many households."

## Why families sit at the top of the table

The survey does not isolate a cause, but the shape of the data is consistent with a straightforward mechanism: **the costs that have risen most are the ones that scale with household size.**

Groceries, utilities, clothing and transport all increase with the number of people in a home, and none offers much room to substitute downward. That constraint shows in the national cutback figures — **67 per cent** of all respondents have cut entertainment and leisure and **40 per cent** have cut personal care, but **32 per cent** have been reduced to trimming essential living expenses including groceries and utilities.

For households with children, the discretionary categories that absorb pressure elsewhere are both smaller and already cut.

Oakes framed the compounding effect: **"When these pressures begin to overlap, households can lose financial flexibility quickly."**

## The back-to-school collision

The timing is the part that makes this more than a data point.

Research published in **July 2026 by the Retail Council of Canada and Caddle** put average back-to-school spending at **$600 to $750 per child** when electronics are included, in a market worth roughly **$4.5 billion** covering about **6 million K-12 students**.

For a two-child household that is **$1,200 to $1,500 landing in a single month** — arriving on top of a group already reporting that 51 per cent are spending more than last year and 42 per cent are using credit for essentials.

One number from that research points to the response most families already use: **only 19 per cent expect to finish back-to-school shopping in a single trip.** Four out of five are already staging the purchase across weeks. Doing that deliberately — buying what is needed for day one now, and deferring clothing, electronics and later-billed school fees — splits the bill across two pay cycles at no cost.

## Support available to households with children

Two things are worth checking before households with children reach for credit.

**The Canada Child Benefit was recalculated in July.** For the **July 2026 to June 2027** period the maximum is **$8,157 a year ($679.75 a month) for a child under 6** and **$6,883 a year ($573.58 a month) for a child aged 6 to 17**, with the maximum paid where adjusted family net income is under **$38,237**. The under-6 amount rose by up to **$160** per child.

The recalculation uses the **2025 tax return**. A family that has not filed does not get recalculated — which makes filing the single highest-value piece of paperwork available to this group.

**Provincial school-supply support varies sharply.** New Brunswick pays a School Supplies benefit once a year in July — for the July 2026 payment, **$100 per child** where 2025 adjusted family net income is **$20,000 or less**, for children born between 1 January 2009 and 31 December 2021. Several provinces provide a school supplies allowance through social assistance. There is no single national programme, so the only reliable step is checking your own province.

## Why this matters

**Credit used for essentials is visible to lenders.** Households where 42 per cent are putting groceries and utilities on credit are producing exactly the bank-data pattern that lenders read across a 60 to 90 day window. It does not automatically prevent approval, but it shapes the affordability assessment.

**The minimum-payment figure compounds.** Thirty-three per cent of households with children expect to make only minimum credit card payments. As **Julie Kuzmic, Head of Consumer Advocacy and Compliance at Equifax Canada**, noted in the release, balances handled that way "can take much longer to repay and cost considerably more in interest."

**Predictable costs deserve different products.** Back-to-school spending is foreseeable and typically falls between $600 and $1,500 — which sits in instalment territory rather than short-term borrowing. It is the one category of family expense that can genuinely be planned around rather than borrowed against at short notice.

## Key takeaways

- **Households with children** report the sharpest pressure of any group in the survey.
- **51 per cent** spending more than a year ago (vs 40% nationally); **42 per cent** using credit for essentials (vs 29%).
- **33 per cent** expect only minimum card payments; **45 per cent** are concerned about supporting family members.
- Back-to-school runs **$600–$750 per child** — and **81 per cent** of parents already spread it across multiple trips.
- **Canada Child Benefit** for July 2026–June 2027: up to **$8,157** (under 6) and **$6,883** (6–17); requires a filed **2025** return.
- Method: **1,532 Canadians aged 18–65**, **July 17–19, 2026**, Leger online panel, **±2.5 per cent, 19 times out of 20**.

## Conclusion

The survey identifies households with children as the group under most strain, and the calendar is about to add to it. But this is also the group with the most available levers — a benefit that was just recalculated upward, provincial supports that go unclaimed, and a seasonal expense that four out of five families already stage rather than pay at once.

The order that helps is unglamorous: file the tax return so the July recalculation reaches you, check what your province offers, buy day-one items now and defer the rest. None of it is dramatic, and together it addresses more of the gap than a short-term loan would.
