**Pension plan membership Canada** wide rose to **7.4 million** active members, Statistics Canada reported in a release published **July 28, 2026** — but the headline gain conceals a divide. Public-sector plans added **157,900** participants, a **4.0%** increase, while private-sector plans **lost 25,800 members**, a decline of **0.8%**. The figures are as of January 1, 2025.

*Reported August 2, 2026, based on Statistics Canada's Pension Plans in Canada release of July 28, 2026, with a January 1, 2025 reference date. This is news reporting and general information, not financial or retirement advice.*

## The 5 key numbers

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Total active members, registered pension plans | **7.4 million** |
| Annual change, all plans | +132,000 (**+1.8%**) |
| Public-sector change | +157,900 (**+4.0%**) |
| Private-sector change | **−25,800 (−0.8%)** |
| Reference date | January 1, 2025 |

The arithmetic is the story. **Total growth was smaller than public-sector growth**, which means the entire national increase — and more — came from public-sector plans, with the private sector subtracting from the total.

## What the pension plan membership Canada split actually shows

Registered pension plans (RPPs) are employer-sponsored retirement plans registered with federal or provincial regulators. Active membership counts people currently accruing benefits, so it is a reasonably direct measure of who is building a workplace pension right now.

On that measure, coverage is becoming concentrated. A 4.0% public-sector gain against a 0.8% private-sector decline widens a gap that has been opening for decades, and it means the experience of retirement planning increasingly depends on which sector a person happens to work in.

Statistics Canada's release reports the totals rather than diagnosing them, and it is worth being precise about what the data does and does not establish. It establishes that fewer private-sector workers were actively accruing RPP benefits. It does not establish that those workers have no retirement savings — group RRSPs, deferred profit sharing plans and individual accounts sit outside the RPP count entirely.

What it does mean is that a growing share of private-sector retirement saving is **voluntary and self-directed**, carrying investment and longevity risk that a pension plan would otherwise pool. Read that way, the pension plan membership Canada figures are less a headcount than a risk-transfer statistic.

## Background: the long shift behind pension plan membership Canada trends

The decline in private-sector coverage is not a 2025 development. It is the continuation of a shift running back decades, as private employers moved first from defined benefit plans — which promise a specified retirement income — toward defined contribution plans, which promise only a contribution, and then in many cases away from registered plans altogether.

Public-sector employment, by contrast, has remained overwhelmingly covered by defined benefit arrangements, and public-sector headcount growth translates directly into pension plan membership Canada totals.

The result is two different retirement systems operating in one country. For the covered worker, retirement income is largely predictable. For the uncovered worker, it depends on personal savings and the public pillars — CPP, OAS and, for lower-income seniors, GIS. The maximum CPP retirement pension is **$1,507.65 per month** in 2026, and most recipients receive considerably less; OAS and GIS amounts are indexed quarterly to inflation.

## Why this matters

Pension plan membership Canada data is usually filed under long-term policy. It has a shorter-term consequence that is already visible in insolvency statistics.

Canadians without a defined benefit pension enter retirement with a **fixed but self-funded** income and no mechanism to increase it. That is precisely the profile that struggles when an unexpected cost arrives, and the debt data reflects it:

- Close to **three in ten** insolvent debtors are aged 50 or older.
- Average unsecured debt among insolvent seniors runs about **$61,000**, including roughly $21,000 in credit card balances.
- Roughly **18%** of Canadians aged 65 and over still carry a mortgage, up from about 10% a decade ago.

For readers still working, the practical implication of the pension plan membership Canada figures is that the default assumption should be *no workplace pension* unless you have confirmed otherwise. Check whether your employer sponsors an RPP, whether it is defined benefit or defined contribution, whether there is an unmatched employer contribution you are leaving on the table, and what your CPP statement of contributions actually projects.

For readers already retired without a workplace pension, the relevant lesson from the insolvency data is about **loan sizing**. On a fixed income there is no way to earn back a shortfall, which is why credit counsellors working with seniors typically recommend keeping total debt payments near 10% of monthly income rather than the 40%-plus that lenders will approve.

## Key takeaways

- **Pension plan membership Canada** wide reached **7.4 million** active members, up 132,000 (+1.8%).
- **All** of that net growth came from the public sector (+157,900, +4.0%); the private sector lost 25,800 members (−0.8%).
- Figures are as of **January 1, 2025** and were published July 28, 2026.
- The data measures registered pension plans only — group RRSPs and individual savings are not counted.
- Retirement without a workplace pension increasingly means self-directed savings plus CPP, OAS and GIS.

## What to watch next

Future pension plan membership Canada releases will show whether the private-sector decline is levelling off or accelerating, and whether public-sector growth continues at 4%. For households, the more immediate signal is the insolvency data, which is published monthly and has been running at levels not seen since 2009.
