**Generative AI Canadian workers** use at work has reached just over one in three, according to Statistics Canada data published **July 30, 2026**. In March 2026, **35.9%** of workers said they had used generative AI tools as part of their main job or business in the past 12 months — while **93.4%** reported being aware such tools exist. It is the first release in what the agency describes as a regular series, drawn from supplementary questions on the Labour Force Survey.

*Reported August 2, 2026, based on Statistics Canada's release of July 30, 2026, covering workers aged 15 to 69 in the March 2026 reference month. This is news reporting and general information, not employment or financial advice.*

![Generative AI Canadian workers using AI tools on a laptop in a modern office](https://images.pexels.com/photos/16094044/pexels-photo-16094044.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## The 5 key numbers

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Aware of generative AI tools | **93.4%** |
| Used the tools in their main job, past 12 months | **35.9%** |
| Familiar with how tools apply to their work | **51.5%** |
| Describe themselves as *very* familiar | **15.0%** |
| Highest-adopting industry | Professional, scientific and technical services, **65.6%** |

## The industry split among generative AI Canadian workers

Adoption is not spread evenly. The gap between the top and bottom sectors is roughly four to one.

| Industry | Used generative AI at work |
|---|---:|
| Professional, scientific and technical services | **65.6%** |
| Finance, insurance, real estate, rental and leasing | **59.2%** |
| Educational services | **53.0%** |
| Agriculture | 17.5% |
| Accommodation and food services | **16.3%** |

The pattern is intuitive: adoption is highest where the work product is documents, analysis and code, and lowest where the work is physical and location-bound. A restaurant server and a management consultant are both workers, but only one of them has a job that a text-generation tool can meaningfully touch.

The more interesting number is the **familiarity gap**. Awareness sits at 93.4% while applied familiarity sits at 51.5%, and genuine confidence — the "very familiar" group — is just 15.0%. Nearly everyone has heard of these tools; only one worker in seven feels they really understand how to apply them to their own job.

## Background: what the generative AI Canadian workers release measures

Statistics Canada gathered this through supplementary questions attached to the Labour Force Survey, covering workers aged 15 to 69 in March 2026. That gives it a large, representative sample and makes it comparable over time as future waves are published.

It is equally important to be clear about the limits. The generative AI Canadian workers data measures **awareness, familiarity and self-reported use**. It does not measure productivity, job displacement, wage effects or hiring. Any claim that these numbers show AI costing Canadians jobs is reading something into the data that is not there.

Statistics Canada has separately published research on Canadian employment trends in the era of generative AI, describing the evidence as early. The honest position in August 2026 is that adoption is measurable and effects are not yet.

## Why this matters for household finances

The link between the growth in generative AI Canadian workers and household borrowing is indirect, and it is worth being precise rather than dramatic about it.

Lenders do not assess whether your industry uses AI. What they assess — particularly the alternative lenders most Canadians turn to outside the big banks — is the **stability and predictability of your deposits** over roughly 90 days. Regular income landing on a regular date reads as low risk. Variable income reads as higher risk regardless of the annual total.

That is where sector change eventually shows up. Industries going through rapid transformation tend to generate more role changes, more contract and project work, and more income variability. Two of the three highest-adopting sectors here — professional services and finance/insurance/real estate — are also sectors where contract and self-employed arrangements are common.

Three practical implications for households:

- **Variable income is a lending problem before it is an income problem.** A worker moving from salary to contract may earn the same annually and still find borrowing harder, because the deposit pattern changed.
- **Buffers matter more in changing sectors.** Statistics Canada's payroll data shows finance and insurance shed 5,700 positions in May 2026 while job vacancies stayed flat nationally at 495,700 — a labour market where switching jobs to fix a budget gap is harder than it was.
- **Familiarity, not just usage, is a career variable.** With only 15.0% of workers describing themselves as very familiar with applying these tools, the gap between awareness and capability is where employability differences are likely to appear first.

None of that is a prediction about job losses. It is an argument for the same thing that was true before this release: a cash buffer and predictable deposits protect households more reliably than forecasts do.

## Key takeaways

- **35.9%** of **generative AI Canadian workers** used the tools in their main job in the past 12 months; **93.4%** are aware of them.
- Only **51.5%** feel familiar with applying them to their work, and just **15.0%** are very familiar.
- Adoption ranges from **65.6%** in professional and technical services to **16.3%** in accommodation and food services.
- The release measures **awareness and use only** — not job losses, wages or productivity.
- This is the **first in a regular series**, so future waves will be more informative than this single reading.

## What to watch next
