Most search results for an **icash review** are written by websites that earn money when you click through and apply, and nearly all of them answer the same question: is this lender good? That is the wrong question, because the price is fixed by federal law and the brand cannot change it. The useful question is what still varies — and the honest answer is that it varies mostly by *your province*, not by the lender.

## Quick answer

Since **January 1, 2025**, every licensed payday lender in Canada is capped at **$14 per $100 borrowed**, all-in. iCash publishes that figure and lends **$100 to $1,500** over **7 to 62 days** in seven provinces. So the price is not a differentiator, the star rating is not a differentiator, and the things that genuinely change your outcome are your province's rules, the term you are given, and what the contract says happens if a payment fails.

## What this icash review can and cannot tell you

There is a limit to what any third party can responsibly claim about a lender, and being straight about that limit is more useful than a score out of five.

**What can be verified:** the published cost of borrowing, the statutory rules that bind every licensed lender in your province, the licence record held by your provincial regulator, and the company's own published terms.

**What cannot be verified by a reviewer:** whether a licence is active on the day *you* apply, what you personally will be approved for, or whether the contract you are handed is compliant. That last one is not hypothetical — Consumer Protection BC found in 2025 that a large, licensed, long-established payday brand had issued more than 5,600 non-compliant contracts. Being a real, licensed lender and issuing a correct contract are two different things.

This article therefore does not issue a verdict. It gives you the facts that are checkable and the method to check the rest yourself. NeedALoanToday is not affiliated with iCash and earns nothing from your decision.

## icash review: the price is set by law, not by the lender

The federal **Criminal Interest Rate Regulations (SOR/2024-114)** exempt payday loans from the 35% criminal rate ceiling only if the loan is under $1,500, runs 62 days or less, is made by a licensed lender in a designated province, and costs **no more than 14% of the advance**. Miss any one of those conditions and it is not legally a payday loan at all.

| What it is | Who sets it | Can the lender change it? |
|---|---|---|
| $14 per $100 cost of borrowing | Federal regulation + province | No — it is a ceiling, not an offer |
| Maximum $1,500 / 62 days | Federal regulation | No |
| Max % of net income per loan | Province | No |
| Cancellation window | Province | No |
| Term length offered | Lender | **Yes** |
| Funding channel and speed | Lender | **Yes** |
| Dishonoured payment fee, default interest | Province sets the ceiling | Up to the ceiling |

iCash's Ontario page states the arithmetic plainly: a **$500 loan costs $70**, repay **$570**. That is the legal maximum. It is also, in practice, what nearly every licensed competitor charges — an academic study of the British Columbia market published in August 2026 found that lenders "priced loans at or very near the cap in each year" across 2012 to 2019, through three separate cap reductions. Price shopping between licensed payday brands is close to pointless by design.

## The province is the product

This is the finding most reviews miss entirely. "iCash" is not one product with one set of rules. It is a separately licensed product in each province, and the consumer protections attached to it differ materially.

| Province | Cost cap | Key borrower protection |
|---|---|---|
| Ontario | $14 / $100 | Max **50% of net income** per loan; **2 business days** to cancel; extended payment plan required on a 3rd loan in 63 days |
| British Columbia | $14 / $100 | Max **50% of a paycheque**; **2 business days** to cancel; no rollovers, one loan at a time |
| Manitoba | $14 / $100 | Max **30% of net income** for the previous calendar month — strictest in Canada; **48 hours** to cancel, measured as a clock |
| Alberta, N.B., N.S., P.E.I. | $14 / $100 | Licensing, cancellation rights and repayment rules vary — confirm on your own regulator's page |

This is why a single icash review score cannot travel across the country: two people borrowing the same $500 from the same brand in Ontario and Manitoba are on measurably different products. The Manitoba borrower's ceiling is calculated on *last month's* income, so a raise this month does not lift it until next month. The Ontario borrower has two business days to cancel; the Manitoba borrower has a 48-hour clock, which on a Friday afternoon expires Sunday afternoon.

## How to date any icash review you read

Here is a test you can apply in ten seconds to this page or any other, and it is the most valuable thing in this article.

**If a review quotes $15 or $17 per $100, it has not been updated since 2024.** Those were old provincial caps. The federal $14 ceiling took precedence on January 1, 2025. At the time of writing, several pages ranking on the first page of Google for this topic still quote $15 per $100 across multiple provinces. A page that is stale on the headline price is very likely stale on everything downstream of it.

Three more freshness markers worth checking:

1. **Does it date any figure it quotes?** Undated numbers in a market that changed in 2025 and again in 2026 are not usable.
2. **Does it disclose whether it is paid when you apply?** Most results competing for this keyword are affiliates or lead generators.
3. **Does it tell you to verify the licence yourself, or does it simply assert one?** Licence status is a live record. No reviewer can vouch for it.

## Where the cost actually diverges

The most commonly missed point in any icash review is this one: the $14 is a price for repaying **on time**. It is not a cap on the transaction. Under the federal regulations, a dishonoured payment fee and default interest sit **outside** the 14% calculation entirely.

In Ontario the ceilings are a **$20 maximum dishonoured payment fee** and **2.5% per month, non-compounding**, on the outstanding principal. So a $500 loan that fails once is no longer a $70 loan. Add a bank NSF charge — capped at $10 at federally regulated banks since March 12, 2026 — and a single missed debit changes the arithmetic more than any brand choice ever could.

That is the practical reason to compare *terms* rather than *lenders*. A longer term with instalments landing on real pay dates is worth far more than a brand preference.

## The 5-minute icash review checklist you can run yourself

Everything above is background. This is the part you actually do, and it takes about five minutes on a phone.

1. **Search your provincial register for the operating company**, not the brand name. Registers list legal entities, so if the trade name returns nothing, take the company name from the website footer or the loan agreement. In Ontario, use the province's *Search for a business licence, registration, or appointment* tool.
2. **Read the cost of borrowing box** and check it says **$14 per $100** or less. Anything higher on a new agreement should stop the process.
3. **Find the due date and compare it to your pay date.** It must fall on or after the day you are actually paid. A due date that lands even one day early is how a manageable loan becomes a failed debit.
4. **Find the dishonoured payment and default clauses** before you read the headline fee. In Ontario the ceilings are a $20 fee and 2.5% per month, non-compounding.
5. **Check the amount against your income ceiling** — 50% of net income in Ontario, 50% of a paycheque in B.C., 30% of last month's net income in Manitoba.
6. **Note your cancellation deadline in your calendar** the moment you sign, with a reminder a few hours before it expires.

If any step cannot be completed — the register returns nothing, the cost box is missing, the due date is wrong — that is your answer, and it is a far more reliable one than a rating.

## Before you borrow: the cheaper ladder

The step most people skip before reading an icash review is checking whether they need a payday loan at all. Work down this list in order — each option is materially cheaper than the one below it.

1. **Ask the biller for time.** Utilities, telecoms and municipalities have hardship processes; in Ontario a regulated electricity utility must issue an overdue notice at least seven days before a disconnection notice, which then gives 14 days.
2. **Employer advance or earned wage access**, if your workplace offers it.
3. **Credit union small loan or overdraft** — capped at 35% APR rather than 365%.
4. **A regulated instalment loan** if the shortfall is above $1,500 or will take longer than 62 days to clear.
5. **A licensed payday loan** — last, and only for a genuine timing gap you can close on the stated date.

If you are on your second or third short-term loan this quarter, the problem is not which lender you picked. Ask for the extended payment plan you are entitled to, and speak to a non-profit credit counsellor before borrowing again.

## The bottom line

A useful icash review is mostly a review of Canadian payday regulation, because regulation determines the price, the maximum, the term and your cancellation rights. What is left for any brand to compete on is speed, channel and term structure — real differences, but small ones next to the question of whether a 365% product is the right tool at all.

Verify the licence in your own province on the day you apply, read the dishonoured-payment and default clauses before you read the headline fee, and make sure the repayment date is one your budget can actually survive.
